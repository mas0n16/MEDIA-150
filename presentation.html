Apple's Domination · 1998 03 / 13
Chapter One

Chic.
Not Geek.

The 1998 iMac campaign showed why it was different from the number one alternative — the PC. Claiming chic is different than anyone else selling technology at the time. Not the most powerful. Just cool.

Apple has long used negative space to create a sense of luxury behind their products and advertising. Negative space was a powerful tool for Apple in redefining the target audience for technology.

Simplicity is luxury.

White space → importance → restraint signals value

Design Principles · Framework 09 / 13
Chapter Seven

So how do they actually do it?

That feeling of rightness isn't magic. It's five design principles, stacked on top of each other, working at the same time. Here they are — then we'll look at the two biggest ones up close.

Principle 01

Anthropo­morphism.

The interface acts alive. Objects have weight, bounce, and memory — so your brain treats them like things, not pixels.

Principle 02

Negative and Positive Space.

Negative space as status symbol. Restraint tells you the product is confident before you've even used it.

Principle 03

Variety.

One goal, many entry points. Different colors, products, target audiences — so every viewer sees themselves with the brand.

Principle 04

Color.

Warm, friendly hues used sparingly. Color signals approachability — "technology for people who don't feel techy."

Principle 05

Simplicity.

Strip until nothing else can be removed. Fewer decisions for the user = more trust, less anxiety, faster flow.

Principle 01 · Close Read 10 / 13
Principle One

Objects
with weight.

Anthropomorphism is when we give human qualities to non-human things. Apple does this with the interface itself. Icons bounce when tapped. Pages rubber-band when you pull too far. Windows scale open from wherever you clicked.

None of these are necessary. But they make the interface feel alive — and your brain, which evolved to recognize living things, responds emotionally before it even thinks.

A button that springs back is a button that seems to want to be pressed again.
Bounce · Squash · Stretch
WeightElements have inertia — they don't teleport.
MemoryGestures reverse cleanly — nothing is lost.
TouchHaptic taps confirm every press, like a handshake.
VoiceSounds — click, swoosh, ding — reward success.
Principles 03 · 04 · 05 11 / 13
The Other Three

Variety, color,
and less.

These three work together as one emotional message: you belong here, and this will be easy.

Variety widens the door — so anyone can walk in. Color makes the door warm and inviting, not cold and technical. Simplicity makes the hallway short — one clear path, nothing to trip over.

Notice that none of these are about features. They're all about how the first five seconds feel.

Variety.

One goal, many entry points. Different colors, products, target audiences — so every viewer sees themselves with the brand.

Color.

Warm accent on neutral canvas. Color is used like punctuation — rarely, deliberately, to guide the eye and signal friendliness.

Simplicity.

Strip until nothing else can be removed. Every subtraction is a gift of attention back to the user. The empty space is the design.
