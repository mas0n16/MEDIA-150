What is Gamification?

Gamification was coined in 2002 by Nick Pelling. It is the application of typical elements of game playing — point scoring, competition with others, rules of play — to other areas of activity, typically as an online marketing technique to encourage engagement with a product or service.

Common applications from the lecture

  • Gamification of free time / casual gaming
  • Gamification of military training (e.g., recruiting games like America's Army)
  • Gamification of cultural exchange

Critique of Gamification — Eric Zimmerman

In strip mining the superficial aspects of games — points, levels, and rewards — gamification leaves the soul of play behind. All of the most valuable experiences of games evaporate when games become weaponized as frequent flyer reward programs. Eric Zimmerman, The Rules We Break

Zimmerman ties gamification to a larger cultural trend that treats humans as rational actors and growth as the only measure of success. He challenges designers to ask:

Key question

"What is your design's intrinsic model for what it means to be human?"

Ludology vs. Narratology

This is one of the central dichotomies in game studies — framed as "games vs. stories." Jesper Juul (Half-Real) notes that game studies are full of these unresolved dichotomies: games vs. players, rules vs. fiction, games vs. stories, games vs. broader culture, and game ontology vs. aesthetics.

Narratology

  • Borrows analytical tools from literature and film to understand video games.
  • Roots go back to Aristotle's Poetics and the study of drama, novels, and film.
  • Today, "narrative" is used broadly — we make sense of the world through narratives (science, national ideology, personal life are all narratively structured).
  • Janet Murray, Hamlet on the Holodeck (1997): argues we desire a fully immersive environment (like the Star Trek holodeck) to engage with a compelling story.

Ludology

  • Broadly means "the study of games" as something distinct — methods from older art forms don't capture what's most important about games, especially play.
  • The term "ludic" comes from Johan Huizinga's 1938 book Homo Ludens. Huizinga argued play is at the center of what makes us human — play is primary to and a necessary condition for culture.
  • "Ludic activity" = showing spontaneous and undirected playfulness.
  • From the Latin ludus (sport), referring to a range of fun activities: games, sports, stage shows, even jokes.

How the dichotomy has softened

The split was much sharper when video games first emerged as a field of study. Today, theorists look for bridges between the two camps. Examples that bridge ludology and narratology:

  • Cutscenes — provide unifying logic and reward the player's actions.
  • Quests — give a sequence of events the player must actualize/enact.

Rules vs. Fiction

A related contradiction worth knowing. Some theorists argue rules are what make a game a game — fiction is incidental. Their argument:

  1. Rules are what makes a game a game.
  2. Fiction is incidental to whether something is a game.
  3. A game can be interesting without fiction.
  4. A game with an interesting fictional world can still be a terrible game.
  5. Therefore, fiction in games is unimportant.

Counter-argument — the Space Invaders thought experiment

Imagine two games with nearly identical rule systems, both similar to Space Invaders:

  • Game 1: shoot down falling fast-fashion clothes → critique of the fast fashion industry's ecological harm.
  • Game 2: shoot down surveillance drones → anti-war / anti-surveillance message.
Takeaway

Same rule system, completely different meaning based on fiction. Rules support fiction allegorically.

Modern game theory treats rules and fiction as sharing varying degrees of importance — fiction cues players into understanding rules, and rules cue players into imagining a fictional world.

Games of Emergence vs. Progression

The history of video games can be seen as the product of two basic game structures.

Games of Emergence

  • The primordial / oldest game structure.
  • A small number of rules combine to yield a large game tree (many possible variations).
  • Players deal with this by designing strategies.
  • Almost all multiplayer games are games of emergence.
  • "Emergence" = a higher-level pattern resulting from interaction between many lower-level entities (like life, consciousness, bird flocks).
  • Example: Pong
  • Test: If a game guide is a strategy guide (rules of thumb for how to play), it's emergence.

Games of Progression

  • Traditional adventure games, loosely based on the fantasy genre (Tolkien-style).
  • Strong control by the game designer — the designer controls the sequence of events.
  • This is where you find games with cinematic or storytelling ambitions.
  • Test: If a game guide is a walk-through (step-by-step), it's progression.

Hybrids — games blend the two

  • Progression games with emergent components: single-player action games where you traverse areas that can each be approached multiple ways.
  • Emergence games with progression components: multiplayer role-playing games where the overall structure is emergent but contains small linear quests.

Jesse Schell's Four Basic Elements

From The Art of Game Design by Jesse Schell. All four work together, but you must be able to separate them when designing or analyzing a game.

1. Mechanics

  • The procedures and rules of the game.
  • The goal and how players can (or can't) achieve it.
  • Mechanics are the crucial difference between games and other (linear) art forms like books and movies, which include the other three elements but not mechanics.
  • The technology must support the mechanics.
  • Example: Death Stranding by Hideo Kojima — turns the mechanic of walking into a meaningful, skill-based core experience instead of a mindless chore.

2. Story

  • The sequence of events that unfolds in the game.
  • Can be linear and pre-scripted OR branching and emergent.

3. Technology

  • Doesn't need to be high-tech — anything from paper and pencil to plastic objects to VR goggles and haptic gloves.
  • Technology enables certain behaviors and prohibits others.
  • It is the medium in which aesthetics happen, mechanics occur, and story is told.

4. Aesthetics

  • The visual style and sonic world of the game.
  • Has the most direct connection to the player's experience.
  • Technology amplifies aesthetics.
Designer's questions (per Schell)

Could my design be improved by enhancing one or more of the categories?

Are the four elements in harmony, reinforcing each other toward a common theme?

Case Study: Space Invaders

Be ready to identify each element in this classic.

  • Technology: Custom motherboard designed specifically for the game — first game to let a player fight an advancing army.
  • Mechanics: Shoot advancing aliens that shoot back; hide behind destructible shields; bonus points for the flying saucer; no time limit; closer aliens = easier/fewer points, farther aliens = more points; more aliens destroyed → invading army speeds up (builds tension).
  • Story: Doesn't need a story, but the story shapes the experience. Taito didn't want soldiers, so the enemies became aliens descending on your planet — you stop them from touching down.
  • Aesthetics: Three different alien designs worth different points; two-frame marching animation; B&W display with colored translucent strips (green ship/shields, red saucer); marching aliens made a heartbeat-like sound that sped up as they did; crunch SFX on hit; cabinet art set the mood.

Colossal Cave Adventure (1975)

Creators: Will Crowther (programmer at Bolt Beranek and Newman / BBN — the company contracted to help build the ARPANET) and his wife Patricia Crowther (Cave Research Foundation cavers — Mammoth Cave, Kentucky, the world's longest cave). Significantly expanded by Don Woods in 1976.

You are in a maze of twisty little passages, all alike.

Why it matters — significance

  1. Considered a foundational text of computer culture and a foundational moment for video games and interactive stories.
  2. Birth of the text adventure / interactive fiction genre — players had to type commands to navigate a subterranean world.
  3. Spread virally over the ARPANET — Will saved the compiled game on a BBN computer before leaving for a month-long vacation. Because BBN's computer was on the new network, the game was discovered by people across the ARPANET. "Where Patricia linked caves, Will linked nodes." This is one of the earliest examples of software spreading through a network.
  4. Origin of interactive narrative in games — players drew their own hand-drawn maps of the world Will described, making it a collaborative imaginative experience.
  5. Described as "as unforgiving as caving itself" — maddening to navigate, addictive to play, called a "harrowing of Hell."

Lineage of non-linear / interactive storytelling

  • Jorge Luis Borges, "The Garden of Forking Paths" (1941) — non-linear narrative, source of the term "Borgesian". Imagines an infinite series of forking, parallel times.
  • Edward Packard (b. 1931) — invented Choose Your Own Adventure with Sugarcane Island (1969). Reader navigates a shipwrecked island, many possible endings, escape requires correct choices.
  • These non-linear story structures are now appearing in television culture (e.g., interactive episodes).
