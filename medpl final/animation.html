Animation

From chronophotography to CGI

Study guide questions

Describe the contributions of Étienne-Jules Marey, J. Stuart Blackton, and Winsor McCay to animation. Explain the principles 'Squash and Stretch,' 'Slow in and Slow out,' and 'Timing.' Explain stop motion, rotoscoping, 3D animation, algorithmic animation, and cutout animation.

Key Contributors to Animation

Étienne-Jules Marey (1830–1904)

Note

Marey is on the study guide but does not appear in the lecture slides. The information below is the standard textbook framing — confirm with your class notes for the version your professor emphasized.

  • French scientist and chronophotographer — invented the chronophotographic gun (1882), a camera capable of taking 12 frames per second of a moving subject on a single photographic plate.
  • Captured sequential movement (birds in flight, humans walking, horses galloping) as a series of still images on one strip — a direct precursor to motion pictures and animation.
  • Significance: Marey's sequential-image studies established the scientific basis for the persistence of vision principle — that a rapid sequence of slightly different still images creates the illusion of continuous motion. This is the foundation of all animation.
  • Often discussed alongside Eadweard Muybridge, who did similar motion studies in the US (e.g., the galloping horse, 1878).

J. Stuart Blackton (early 1900s)

  • Filmmaker who used stop-motion and drawn animations in silent films in the early 1900s.
  • Notable works:
    • The Enchanted Drawing (1900)
    • The Haunted House (1907)
  • Significance: One of the earliest pioneers of film animation — bridging the gap between live-action film and animated drawings.

Winsor McCay (1914)

  • Created Gertie the Dinosaur (1914).
  • Significance — the big takeaway:
    • Introduced character personality in animation. Gertie had discernible emotional responses, individual personality traits, and behaviors beyond basic gestures.
    • Employed technical advances like animated loops and key frames.
  • Disney built on McCay's emotional storytelling innovation — realizing emotionally gripping stories were the key to animated film success.
Disney's three innovations

Walt Disney built on McCay by: (1) being an early adopter of creative sound in animation, (2) developing the "Story Department" to elevate narrative quality and create memorable characters, and (3) understanding that animation could be feature-length entertainment, not just comedic shorts (e.g., Snow White, 1937, the first American feature-length animated film).

The Three Principles You Need

From Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas, The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation. Note: the lecture spells "Johnson" but the correct name is Johnston.

Squash and Stretch

Gives a sense of weight, volume, and flexibility to animated objects.

Think of a bouncing ball: it squashes (flattens) on impact and stretches (elongates) as it moves. Without this, objects feel rigid and lifeless. It tells the viewer about the physical properties of what's being animated.

Slow In and Slow Out

Objects in the real world tend to slowly accelerate and slowly decelerate.

In motion graphics and video editing programs, this is sometimes referred to as "ease in" / "ease out." Mimics natural physics — almost nothing in the real world starts or stops instantly. Without this, motion feels mechanical and robotic.

Timing

More frames = slower motion. Fewer frames = faster motion.

The example shown in lecture: an animation that uses just one frame at the fastest moment in the center — demonstrating how frame count directly controls perceived speed. Timing controls the rhythm, pace, and weight of the animation.

Bonus principles from the lecture

  • Arcs: Almost all actions follow an arc/circular path — gives more natural action and better flow.
  • Straight Ahead vs. Pose to Pose:
    • Straight Ahead: draw each frame one after another.
    • Pose to Pose: use key frames as starting/stopping points, with the in-between frames interpolated. In Adobe Premiere/After Effects this is keyframing; in Flash it was called "tweening" (automatically generated in-between frames).

Types of Animation

Stop Motion

Definition: Take a shot, make a slight movement or modification of the subject, then take another shot. When viewed in sequence, this creates the illusion of motion.

  • Examples cited: The Quay Brothers; Jan Švankmajer's Darkness, Light, Darkness (1989).
  • Works with any physical subject — clay, puppets, objects, even people.

Rotoscoping

Definition: The tracing of an image from a film one frame at a time to create an animation.

  • Invented by Max Fleischer, patented in 1917.
  • Originally: animators used a rotoscope to project the image onto a frosted glass panel and trace.
  • Now done digitally, but the concept is the same — using video/film footage as the basis to trace the subject.
  • Examples: Richard Linklater's Waking Life (feature film); Undone (Amazon series, 2019); Joel Haver's YouTube animations using EbSynth.

3D Animation (CGI — Computer-Generated Imagery)

Definition: Animation in a virtual 3D environment with control over width, height, and depth (x, y, z axes).

Key features from the lecture

  • Models built from geometrical vertices.
  • Rigging: giving control points and handles to objects — "like a very complicated marionette." Woody in Toy Story had approximately 700 control points.
  • Control over lighting (spot lights = stage lights; point lights = light bulbs) to accentuate depth.
  • Control over textures and materials on object surfaces.
  • A 3D camera can dolly through the scene on any axis.
  • Example cited: Chris Landreth's Ryan (3D animated documentary).

Algorithmic Animation

Definition: An animation that uses the procedural and spatial affordances of a computer to execute many repeated commands that control placement, line, shape, vector, or other design elements to create the illusion of on-screen movement.

Key idea

It's code-driven — the animator writes rules/commands, and the computer generates the visual movement procedurally rather than the animator drawing each frame.

Cutout Animation

Definition: A type of stop-motion animation that uses flat materials cut from different sources — magazines, photography, cards, fabric.

  • "Think of cutout animation like a photomontage in motion."
  • Contemporary cutouts are often done digitally.
  • Examples cited:
    • Stan VanDerBeek pioneered what he called "Cut and Paste Cinema" — combining collage with animation, inter-splicing live footage, scratching and painting directly on celluloid (e.g., Science Friction, 1959).
    • Terry Gilliam's animations in Monty Python's Flying Circus.
    • Lemony Snicket's opening sequences.
