Janet Murray's Four Affordances

Source: Janet Murray, Inventing the Medium and Hamlet on the Holodeck.

Together, these four affordances mean the computer can embody rules and execute them; allows us to manipulate its objects; can contain more information in more forms than any previous medium; and can create a world we can navigate, inhabit, and observe.

1. Encyclopedic

  • Definition: Unequalled storage + infinite library (both technological and cultural). The computer can contain more information in more forms than any previous medium.
  • Examples to maximize this affordance: databases, archives, encyclopedias.
  • Art example: Listening Post by Mark Hansen & Ben Rubin — visualizes and sonifies an internet chatroom from the early 2000s. The encyclopedic affordance is central; it draws on a vast pool of live online conversation.

2. Spatial

  • Definition: Navigating + GUI (graphic user interface). Creates worlds we can move through, inhabit, and observe.
  • Examples to maximize this affordance: maps, GPS, navigation conventions, information spaces.
  • Art example: Kinfolk by Idris Brewster & Glenn Cantave — uses augmented reality to render new monuments of Black and Brown icons (e.g., Toussaint Louverture replacing the Columbus statue at Columbus Circle).

3. Procedural

  • Definition: Computer processes. The ability to calculate sequences of mathematical calculations — like executing a recipe, but where every step is a precise computation (no "dash of salt").
  • An algorithm is a set of rules to be followed in sequence.
  • Named after Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (astronomer, head of the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, 820–850) — developed foundations of algebra; the term algebra derives from his name.
  • Examples to maximize this affordance: game engines, search engines, spreadsheets, control conventions.
  • Art example: The Good Life (Enron Simulator) by Sam Lavigne & Tega Brain — recreates the experience of receiving all 500,000 emails from the Enron archive over a chosen timescale.

4. Participatory

  • Definition: Human interaction. Allows us to manipulate the computer's objects.
  • Examples to maximize this affordance: social media, blogs, recommendation systems, input devices, icon/command conventions.
  • Art example: Conversations with Bina48 by Stephanie Dinkins — highlights the participatory affordance through dialogue with an AI.

Lev Manovich's Principles of New Media

Source: Lev Manovich, The Language of New Media.

Manovich asks a slightly different question than Murray: What is 'new' about new media? How is it different from traditional media?

1. Numerical Representation

  • All new media objects are made of digital code (0s and 1s) and can be described as a mathematical function, then subjected to algorithmic manipulation (programming).
  • Media becomes programmable.
  • Because all new media share the same binary code, mappings can be made between once-discrete mediums — data can be mapped between text, sound, image, and film.
  • Example: Andrew Wilkinson's Lord's Prayer in Binary Code; Andrew Demirjian's The Week in Review maps a week of stock market data of the three largest music industry corporations to music and visuals.

2. Modularity

  • A new media object consists of independent parts, each of which consists of smaller independent parts. These parts can be modified independently.
  • Trying to do this in the analog realm would require redoing the entire piece.
  • Example: In an animation/code piece, the "cars" element can be swapped out and changed to "trains" without affecting the rest of the code.

3. Automation

  • Repeated tasks can be operated by the computer that are involved in media creation, manipulation, and access.
  • Note: Murray's "Procedural" affordance is referred to as Automation in Manovich's principles — they describe the same thing.
  • Example: The "Actions" feature in Photoshop — records and executes a sequence of steps (tonal adjustment, cropping) across a batch of images. Also: Pentametron by Ranjit Bhatnagar — a Twitter bot that automatically finds and retweets tweets in iambic pentameter.

4. Variability

  • The new media object can exist in an unlimited number of states.
  • Examples: Websites customized to the user in real time (your Amazon homepage is unique to you); interactive installations that change based on user engagement; Question Bridge — each person's trip through the work will most likely be different.

5. Transcoding

  • New media operates on two distinct layers:
    • Cultural layer — structural organization that makes sense to human users (images of sunsets, text forming sentences).
    • Computer layer — follows the computer's conventions and data organization (lists, records, arrays).
  • Example: The color white — we understand it culturally as "white," but the computer represents it as a specific combination of three values (R, G, B from 0–255).
  • Manovich argues the two layers influence each other, creating a blend of human and computer meanings: "Traditional ways in which human culture modeled the world and the computer's own means of representing it are coming together."
  • Analog objects only have one level; new media has two.

Responsive Environments

Definition: Media artworks that rely on an exchange between the physical world and media. The works are participatory and engage with a user, OR they respond to an ecosystem/event in the physical world. They foster a symbiotic exchange — like cells — between the physical and mediated worlds.

Key Characteristics

  • Built on an exchange between physical world and digital media
  • Participatory — engage with users or respond to physical events
  • Symbiotic — physical and mediated worlds work together
  • Feel organic — replicate human engagement with the world
  • Often built using Arduino (open-source electronics prototyping platform) and sensors (light, motion, pressure, temperature, infrared, accelerometers) and platforms like Raspberry Pi

The Three Examples on Your Study Guide

A Father's Lullaby — Rashin Fahandej

  • Touch-activated audio portraits.
  • Intimate interviews, songs, and lullabies offer poetic meditations on the spaces of love and trauma, presence and absence.
  • Highlights the role of men in raising children and their absence due to racial disparities in the criminal justice system and the direct impact on children, women, and lower-income communities.
  • Uses personal memories to interrogate the structural violence of mass incarceration.

Simmer Down Sprinter — Steve Lambert

  • Maps fluctuations in the user's fingers' Galvanic Skin Response (GSR) — the change in the electrical resistance of the skin caused by emotional stress (same metric used in lie-detector tests) — to make runners on a 1/4-mile virtual oval track move faster or slower.
  • The more the player relaxes (lowering their GSR), the faster the runner on screen moves and wins the race.
  • Rewards the player through focused relaxation — a biofeedback artwork.

Omnia per Omnia — Sougwen Chung

  • Collaboration between Sougwen Chung & D.O.U.G._L.A.S. (Drawing Operations Unit: Generation_3 Live Autonomous System) — a swarm of custom-designed drawing robots.
  • Reimagines landscape painting as a collaboration between an artist, a robotic swarm, and the dynamic flow of a city.
  • Explores the poetics of various modes of sensing: human and machine, organic and synthetic, improvisational and computational.
  • A speculation on new pluralities — the composite agency of human + machine.

Parallel Tendencies Between Art & Computer Science

Theorist: Söke Dinkla — proposed that new media could be seen as parallel tendencies between modern art and computer science since the end of WWII.

Similar ideas and concepts were being articulated at roughly the same times by artists and computer scientists. They were even using the same forms to deliver their messages: manifestos (theoretical programs by computer scientists) and completed artworks (prototypes to demonstrate ideas by artists).

What Both Domains Share — Art as:

  • Temporal process (not an object)
  • Active participation of the audience
  • Algorithm (instruction sets)
  • Indeterminate
  • Chance events (randomness in programming)

Computer Science Innovations of the Era

  • Ivan Sutherland — created Sketchpad in 1964, a major innovation in human-computer interaction (HCI).
  • Douglas Engelbart — "The Mother of All Demos," Dec. 9, 1968 — introduced the computer mouse, video conferencing, hypertext, word processing, and dynamic file linking.

Artists Doing Parallel Work

Sol LeWitt — Wall Drawings (Conceptual Art)

  • His instruction-set works can be easily turned into computer code.
  • Writing out in plain text what you want a program to do is called pseudocode — Sol LeWitt's Work from Instructions (1971) is essentially pseudocode.
  • Example instruction from 1971: "USING A BLACK, HARD CRAYON DRAW A TWENTY INCH SQUARE. DIVIDE THIS SQUARE INTO ONE INCH SQUARES. WITHIN EACH ONE INCH SQUARE, DRAW NOTHING, OR DRAW A DIAGONAL STRAIGHT LINE FROM CORNER TO CORNER OR TWO CROSSING STRAIGHT LINES DIAGONALLY FROM CORNER TO CORNER."
  • Practiced combinatorial aesthetics — using permutations (arranging all members of a set into some sequence or order) — exactly like a computer program iterating through possibilities.

Yoko Ono — Event Scores (Fluxus)

  • Conversation Piece (1962), Snow Piece (1963), Cut Piece (1965).
  • Event scores = written instructions for performances. Like Sol LeWitt's wall drawings, these are essentially algorithms — sets of rules to be followed by the performer/audience.

Fluxus (broader context)

  • Founded in 1960 by George Maciunas (Lithuanian/American artist).
  • Began as a small international network of artists/composers; characterized as a shared attitude rather than a movement.
  • No single unifying style — DIY, used whatever materials were at hand.
  • Democratic, open to anyone; valued simplicity, anti-commercialism, chance, accident, humor.
  • Encouraged collaboration between artists, across artforms, and with the audience.
  • Key figures: Joseph Beuys, Yoko Ono, Nam June Paik, Ben Vautier, Dick Higgins, Alice Hutchins, Robert Watts.
What these disparate domains share

Both artists and computer scientists in the 1960s were converging on the same core ideas:

  • Art/code as instructions (Sol LeWitt's wall drawings, Yoko Ono's event scores = algorithms)
  • Process over object (a wall drawing is the execution of instructions; a program is the execution of code)
  • Active audience participation (Fluxus pieces require performers; software requires users)
  • Chance and indeterminacy (Fluxus randomness; programmatic randomness)
  • Modularity and recombination (permutation poems; combinatorial code)

This explains why new media isn't simply a product of engineering — it emerged from a wider cultural moment where artists and scientists were independently arriving at the same conceptual breakthroughs.
