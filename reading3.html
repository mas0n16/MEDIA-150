Graphic Novel: Motherhood in Crisis

Reading 3 Response

"Motherhood in Crisis" is not the kind of piece you scroll past quickly. The graphic novel format of illustrated panels, minimal text, four women's faces carrying the weight of the story forces you to slow down in a way that a standard article wouldn't. That's clearly intentional. When the subject is maternal death, making the reader linger feels like the whole point.

The stat that opens the piece is the one that stays with you: in Sierra Leone, one in ten women die during childbirth. That number is staggering on its own, but what the graphic novel format does is put a face, a name, and a specific journey behind it. Memunatu traveled from a remote village just to reach basic care. Heela, a schoolgirl, whose pregnancy derails whatever future she was building. Kadiatu grieving in a way no mother should have to. Tetteh navigating pregnancy inside a prison. Four completely different circumstances, all arriving at the same systemic failure.

What struck me was how the comic form handles emotion differently than journalism does. There's no expert quoted, no statistics broken down in a chart. Instead you're just watching these women's expressions, their bodies, the spaces around them. It's a deliberate choice to lead with humanity first and context second, and it works. You feel the isolation before you understand the policy failures behind it.

It also made me think about who this kind of storytelling is designed to reach. A long investigative piece on maternal mortality in West Africa might get read by people already interested in global health. A graphic novel gets shared, gets looked at, gets felt by people who wouldn't have clicked otherwise. The medium is doing real advocacy work here, not just documentation.

The hardest part of reading this was sitting with the fact that these aren't edge cases. Sierra Leone's healthcare system has been hit by Ebola, then COVID-19, and the women absorbing the consequences of that collapse are the ones least responsible for it. That disconnect between who causes a crisis and who suffers through it is what the piece quietly builds toward without ever saying it directly. That restraint is what makes it land so successfully.
