Extra Credit: Nancy Baker Cahill

Guest Speaker Response

Nancy Baker Cahill was not what I expected from a guest speaker. Most presentations like this follow a more predictable format — this is my work, this is my process, this is what it means to me and the impact I hope to have. Cahill came in with something closer to a philosophy, and the art was almost secondary to the framework she built around it. By the end I wasn't just thinking about her pieces, I was thinking about consciousness, about what we should be doing for each other, and all the great things that technology can do for mankind.

Her most prominent medium that we learned about is augmented reality, but that description kind of undersells it. What she's really doing is bringing visibility of the unseen. The 4th Wall platform she founded is free and borderless by design — she made a point of saying that art and its uses cannot be contained, referencing work she's done at the US-Mexico border. AR is the right tool for that mission because it doesn't need you to go anywhere. It floats in the world you already inhabit and changes what you see there. The Mushroom Cloud piece does exactly that — a catastrophic image that transforms mid-air into something more delicate and regenerative, like cells repairing themselves. Cento at the Whitney took it further, creating a fictional interspecies creature that visitors could collectively add to through the app, sharing pieces that they believe should have more focus in the world. The work only becomes itself through participation.

What fascinated me most was the thinking underneath the visuals. Cahill talked about resonance phenomenon — the idea that brain waves and heartbeats can create a kind of shared consciousness. Collective humming as a literal example of quantum connection. She framed each video as a chapter in a book, which made me think about how she sees her whole body of work as one long argument rather than a collection of separate pieces. The LA fires came up too — she talked about working before, during, and after them, treating fire as essential material, almost like DNA. There was something about the possibility of measuring a planet's changing climate through sound that was fascinating to hear about.

The line that hit the hardest for me was this: she said she doesn't worry that AI will destroy humanity, but rather the humanity within us. The threat isn't some external force — it's distraction, inertia, the handful of people who profit from keeping us disengaged. She brought up Marshall McLuhan's hot and cold media in that context, the idea that different media demand different levels of participation from the audience. AR, the way she uses it, is deliberately cold in McLuhan's sense as it requires you to show up, move, and engage.

What I kept coming back to is how much her work is about care. Regenerative systems, biomimicry, the idea that we are all we've got. they're ethical points that are important to consider and ponder. She's building platforms and experiences that assume interdependence as a starting point rather than something to be argued for. Coming from a background in luxury and brand experience, I think about immersion a lot differently than most people in those spaces do. Cahill made me realize that the most powerful immersive experiences aren't the ones that impress you — they're the ones that make you feel like you belong to something larger than yourself. That's a standard worth spreading in this world.
