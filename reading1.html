In Plato's Cave by Susan Sontag

Reading 1 Summary


Susan Sontag's "In Plato's Cave" captures the way that we see photography as an extension of the world and our consciousness connected to it. We use photography to hold onto specific instances in time, whether the experience be personal or a framework of the outside world. She says "to collect photographs is to collect the world" and that "the most grandiose result of the photographic enterprise is to give us the sense that we can hold the whole world in our heads – as an anthology of images". As a society, we have shifted to capturing and living for the photograph in one way or another. Photographs are experiences captured, and the camera is an arm of consciousness as Sontag mentions. Photography can be a message, a work of art, a feeling of productivity, and all these things molded to one. It has become one of the principal devices for experiencing something and for giving an appearance of participation. She has an interesting view on the act of taking a picture of others, saying there is something predatory in this. In some ways "to photograph people is to violate them, by seeing them as they never see themselves, by having knowledge of them they can never have; it turns people into objects that can be symbolically possessed. Just as the camera is a sublimation of a gun, to photograph someone is a sublimated murder–a soft murder, appropriate to a sad, frightened time". I thought this view was interesting to place on a photographer. It can be argued that this photographic violation is one of the most important forms of photography as a whole–to capture and spread awareness of violence in times of war or cruelty. She even argues that eventually, people might learn to act out more of their aggressions more with cameras and less with guns, such as mentioning the replacement of guns with cameras in the gun safari in East Africa. A photo can also be an important extension of one's memory in times of grief or sorrow. Sontag famously mentions that "a photograph is both a pseudo-presence and a token of absence. Like a wood fire in a room, photographs–especially those of people, of distant landscapes and faraway cities, of the vanished past–are incitements to reverie". Reminiscing and daydreaming over a photo of a lost loved one or a sentimental time in the past has become a natural sense of photography as an extension of our consciousness. Photographs are attempts to tune to another reality and can abet desire in the most direct and rewarding way. Sontag has a way of expressing the consciousness and effect of photography in such an important way. A photograph holds a piece of history but also a snapshot of the photographer's feeling and emotion at a particular moment in their personal human experience. When the author mentions that "the ultimate wisdom of the photographic image is to say: there is the surface. Now think–or rather feel, intuit–what is beyond it, what the reality must be like if it looks this way", she is trying to express to the reader the process in which we view an image. We have a natural way of examining the world from our subconscious in everything we see, but viewing from another lens can be transformative in the way we understand reality. Photography can be an extremely powerful tool in encouraging a more humanistic and conscious way of seeing.
